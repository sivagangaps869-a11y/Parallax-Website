Iconic Design

The architectural identity of Jumeirah Burj Al Arab is rooted in the region’s maritime heritage. Tasked with designing the building, British architect Tom Wright of WS Atkins turned to the iconic shape of an Arabian dhow sail. One evening, he sketched the first outline on a napkin while sitting at the once then Chicago Beach hotel – a simple drawing that would evolve into the 321-meter-tall structure known today. Constructed on a man-made island 280 metres from the coastline, the hotel required pioneering engineering. More than 230 massive concrete piles, each driven 40 meters deep into the seabed, support the island – a foundation that ensures the building remains resilient against shifting sand and tides. The result is a superstructure that appears to float above the sea, connected to the mainland by a private curving causeway. Inside, the design is equally dramatic. The atrium, the tallest of its kind in the world, rises over 180 metres, its vertical sweep accentuated by towering golden columns and a kaleidoscope of geometric colour. It is a space that feels both theatrical and palatial, its scale echoing the ambition that shapes the entire project. The interiors, led by celebrated designer Khuan Chew of KCA International of a design competition, combine Arabesque influences with bold contemporary luxury. Over 30 different types of Statuario marble - the same prized stone used by Michelangelo – cloak nearly 24,000 square metres of flooring and walls. Approximately 1,790 square metres of 24-carat gold leaf shimmer across interior surfaces, catching light in subtle, glowing layers. Even the smallest details were made to dazzle: 86,500 hand-fixed Swarovski crystals embellish mirrors and features within the hotel.

The Art of Service
The hotel’s architecture may turn heads, but it is the human touch that defines its soul. At Jumeirah Burj Al Arab, service is elevated to an art form. Guests are greeted with the hotel’s signature Marhaba ritual: rose water, refreshing cold towels, dates, and aromatic Arabic coffee – a gesture rooted in centuries-old Arabian hospitality. Each suite is assigned a dedicated butler trained to anticipate needs before they arise. On grounds, Musafir, the hotel’s resident camel whose name means ‘traveller’ in Arabic, offers guests a symbolic walk, a nod to the desert journeys that once connected this region. For travel across the city, a fleet of Rolls-Royces stand ready ensuring that even a simple airport transfer feels like an extension of the hotel’s rarefied world.
